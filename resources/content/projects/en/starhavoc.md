# A Game I Always Wanted to Build

For years I wanted to build a game. The push came from a browser space game I played for hours as a teenager: it no longer runs on my Mac. So I designed my own. **STARHAVOC** is a space MMO you play in the browser on computer, tablet and phone, with a shared world, factions, clans, story and boss fights.

I handled everything myself: **logo and brand, art direction, game design, story, 3D pipeline, client and server**. The game is live at [starhavoc.net](https://starhavoc.net), so feel free to jump in, pick a faction and fly a round.

## A Broken Star as a Logo

The world starts with one sentence: STARHAVOC is the night the star Kessar broke apart. The mark tells that story. It is a **broken station ring**, an octagon with one plate blasted out of its corner, around a four-pointed star. The loose plate is the only colour in the logo: signal amber.

The **wordmark is built from geometry, not set in a typeface**. Wide capitals as filled outlines, large outer radii with tight inner ones, A and V without crossbars. After the first round I matched the mark to the wordmark: the ring now has the same stroke weight and the same corner radii as the O. Everything is generated in code, one script writes the logos, the favicon, the app icons and the link preview image. A brand guide and an export folder with every lockup in every format document the system. I explored 16 concepts before settling on this one, and dropped ideas like using the ΛV from the wordmark as a secondary motif, because people read it as "AV".

Where the game loads, the mark moves: in **Fracture**, the ring cracks into seven plates, they drift apart around the star and lock back one by one, the amber plate last. Small placeholders get a calmer version where only the plate drifts and snaps back. For the logo intro the plates fly in and lock, then the wordmark builds letter by letter, then the ring breaks once.

## A Shape Language From the Mark

The interface speaks the shape of the logo. **Anything you can press is an octagon or has a chamfer**, like the ring. Panels, windows, buttons and tags take two cut corners. Round shapes are reserved for signals that trigger nothing: online dots, map blips, the touch-stick thumb. No pills, one accent colour, bone white on graphite and deep space.

## A Landing Page in the HUD Look

[starhavoc.net](https://starhavoc.net) opens with a landing page that looks like the game's cockpit: a HUD frame around the page, a star field with parallax, and the logo intro from the brand film, with an amber scan line riding over each letter. A **star map** leads from the home systems to the Shattered Nexus with codex art and an automatic tour, **codex cards** scan themselves in on hover, and even the cursors come from the brand: a bone arrow, an amber pointer, a red reticle over enemies.

## Art Direction: Used Future

The look is angular and worn, a **used future** with crystal swarms in teal and magenta against industrial hulls. Ships follow one pipeline: a **concept painting with Nano Banana**, then a **3D model with meshy or tripo**, then optimisation with **gltf-transform** to WebP textures and under 60,000 triangles per ship. Hardpoints, engine positions and glow sit in a table per model, and if a model is missing, a procedural ship takes its place.

Every ship can be **painted**: hull paint, an accent for light strips, shield and pattern, an engine flame colour and patterns like stripes, chevrons or hex plating. The paint runs as a shader tint on the model, so every combination works on every hull. Some paints are only earned, through mastery ranks, a faction quartermaster or a seasonal boss.

## Building It With Claude Code and Agents

I built STARHAVOC with **Claude Code** as my development partner, in long sessions with parallel agents: one built the drone models, one hunted for security holes like item duplication or reward farming, others ran browser tests on desktop and phone. Through the **Magnific MCP**, concepts, models, illustrations and music came straight out of the conversation. I set the direction and reviewed every concept.

After each playtest I wrote down what felt off, sometimes 25 points at once: jittering ships, abrupt music cuts, a fire button hidden behind a tutorial card on the iPhone. Each round went back into the code with measurements, not guesses.

## Blender by Script

Not everything came out of a generator. Bosses like the **Hive, the Shard Overseer and the Void Herald**, the small swarm enemies, and a kit of 11 weapons and 15 modules are **Blender models written as Python scripts**. Each script builds a low-poly mesh from layered parts, bakes base colour, normals, roughness and emission with Cycles, and exports an optimised model. Stations, jump gates and clan bases get baked hull textures from Blender too. That keeps every model reproducible: change a parameter, run the script, export again.

## Characters, Story and Sound

The game tells its story in **three chapters**, from salvage rights to the final fight against the Devourer. A lore bible holds the cluster of ten systems, the factions and the side characters, and a **codex with 98 entries** fills up as you play. **Thea Marlow, Liv Lindqvist and Idris Vasse** guide you through briefings and radio calls with portraits in the key-art style. Each chapter opens with a short animated intro, and every system has its own music track.

## Real-Time Multiplayer Architecture

Under the hood it is a real online game, **built without a game engine**:

- **Client:** three.js and TypeScript, no framework. The HUD and windows are plain DOM, labels and damage numbers sit on an overlay canvas. Windows load lazily and three.js lives in its own chunk, so the first load dropped from 1.37 MB to 413 kB.
- **Authoritative server:** Node 24 runs TypeScript directly. The client only sends intentions (target, course, purchase). One 20 Hz tick runs every sector, and every pilot gets 10 snapshots per second over WebSocket, compressed from about 7 KB to 1.5 KB per combat snapshot.
- **Shared rules:** content, economy and the protocol live in one shared folder. The server executes them, the client uses them for display.
- **Prediction:** the flight model runs identically on server and client, so your own ship reacts in **5 to 13 ms instead of 94 to 278 ms** at up to 150 ms ping. Every other ship is interpolated.
- **Persistence:** one SQLite database inside the same process, with a daily backup.
- **Hardening:** origin checks, connection and login limits, hashed passwords and session tokens, a content security policy and zero-downtime deploys that save every pilot before a restart.

## Security and Pentests

An open online game attracts bots, cheaters and trolls, so security got the same attention as the gameplay:

- **Accounts:** sign-up with e-mail confirmation, Cloudflare Turnstile against bots, rate limits per IP for connections, logins, registrations and password resets.
- **Chat:** text is never rendered as HTML, and the content security policy only allows the game's own scripts. Messages are capped at 200 characters and 400 ms apart, a filter masks slurs and insults (leetspeak included) and refuses offensive pilot names, and admins can mute and ban.
- **Edge:** Cloudflare with a web application firewall in front of the server.
- **Pentests:** two agents attacked a local instance with the production configuration in parallel, each with its own server and database. One took the web, login and admin surface, the other the game protocol and the economy, hunting for WebSocket abuse and item dupes. Production itself was only checked from the outside, without writing anything: headers, TLS, open paths and direct IP access.

## Built for the Phone, Too

STARHAVOC is a **PWA** with a phone-first HUD: the action buttons sit on two symmetric arcs under your thumb, windows open full screen, and the layout respects the Dynamic Island and the home bar. I measured and tuned the frame rate on phones in combat from 42 to 54 FPS and cut the downloads at login from 42 MB to 14 MB.

## More Than Flying and Shooting

You fly from **two perspectives**: the classic top-down view of the genre, or a **chase camera** right behind your ship, one key away at any moment. The same game, the same server, but the swarm suddenly comes at you instead of crawling across a map.

Fifteen ships with real differences in speed, agility, inertia and armour. Drones with formations and cores. A skill tree, three factions with home systems and reputation, clans with bases, gates with waves and target times, weekly modifiers, a seasonal world boss, duels with Elo ratings, leaderboards, a star map with autopilot across jump gates, and a **photo mode** with a free camera and filters.

## Admin, Design View and Documentation

An admin backend handles pilots, bans, scheduled events and an economy journal. Its **design view** renders every enemy, ship, weapon, explosion and icon under the game's lighting, so I can review art in one place. A system architecture document lives in the admin as well, with the source path next to every number it quotes.

## Tested Like a Product

More than **600 unit, simulation and server tests** and Playwright end-to-end tests cover the game. A progression simulator plays every hunting ground several times to balance level pacing, and dedicated scripts measure jitter, collision and stutter during sector changes.

## The Result

A game I always wanted to make, running on my own server: its own brand, its own world and a multiplayer architecture that holds up on a phone. And finally a space game that runs on my Mac again. Come fly with me at [starhavoc.net](https://starhavoc.net).
